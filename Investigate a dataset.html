Investigate a Dataset: TMDb Movie Data¶

Dataset analyzed: TMDb Movies dataset

Introduction¶

For this project, I used the TMDb movie dataset to look at a few patterns in movie performance and how those patterns change across time and genre. The dataset includes information such as popularity, adjusted budget and revenue, runtime, genres, vote averages, and release year.

One thing I noticed early on is that a lot of the budget and revenue entries are listed as zero. I did not treat those as movies that literally had no budget or revenue. Instead, I treated them as missing financial information so they would not distort the financial part of the analysis.

Questions¶

  1. How are adjusted budget, adjusted revenue, and popularity related for movies that have usable financial data?
  2. How do popularity and runtime change across release decades and genres?

These questions let me compare individual variables first and then look at how several variables relate to one another.

Data Wrangling¶

I started by checking the overall shape of the dataset, the column types, missing values, duplicate rows, and values that looked unusual. The biggest issue for this project was the number of zero values in budget_adj and revenue_adj.

I kept movies with missing financial information in the main cleaned dataset because they can still be useful when looking at popularity, genre, runtime, or release year. For the financial question, I made a separate subset that only includes movies with positive adjusted budget and adjusted revenue values.

Cleaning decisions¶

I made a few cleaning choices before starting the analysis:

  • Removed exact duplicate rows.
  • Removed rows with missing genre values because genre is used in the second question.
  • Removed rows where runtime is zero because a zero-minute movie is not useful for this analysis.
  • Kept the larger cleaned dataset for the non-financial comparisons.
  • Created a separate financial_df with movies that have positive adjusted budget and adjusted revenue values.
  • Added adjusted profit using revenue_adj - budget_adj. I only use this as a simple descriptive measure because the dataset does not include every possible cost tied to a movie.
In [4]:
# Create the main cleaned dataset.
clean_df = df.drop_duplicates().copy()
clean_df = clean_df.dropna(subset=['genres'])
clean_df = clean_df[clean_df['runtime'] > 0].copy()

# Create a financial subset only where both adjusted budget and revenue are reported.
financial_df = clean_df[
    (clean_df['budget_adj'] > 0) &
    (clean_df['revenue_adj'] > 0)
].copy()

# Create an adjusted profit variable for descriptive comparisons.
financial_df['profit_adj'] = (
    financial_df['revenue_adj'] - financial_df['budget_adj']
)

print("Rows in original dataset:", len(df))
print("Rows in cleaned dataset:", len(clean_df))
print("Rows with usable financial data:", len(financial_df))

def label_plot(title, xlabel, ylabel):
    """Add a title and axis labels to the current Matplotlib plot."""
    plt.title(title)
    plt.xlabel(xlabel)
    plt.ylabel(ylabel)
    plt.tight_layout()

Question 2: How do movie popularity and runtime vary across release decades and genres?¶

For this question, I went back to the broader cleaned dataset because budget and revenue are not required. I grouped release years into decades and compared movie counts, popularity, vote averages, and runtime over time.

Before grouping movies by decade and genre, I looked at runtime on its own. This gives me a baseline for the typical spread of movie lengths before comparing groups.

Conclusions¶

Main findings¶

For the first question, movies with usable financial information show a moderate positive relationship between adjusted budget and adjusted revenue. The Pearson correlation in the cleaned financial subset is about 0.57. Popularity and adjusted revenue are also positively related, with a correlation of about 0.55. The relationships are noticeable, but the scatterplots also show a lot of variation. In other words, bigger budgets and higher popularity are often connected with higher revenue in this dataset, but neither one guarantees it.

For the second question, popularity and runtime both change across time and genre. Mean popularity is higher in the more recent decades, while median runtime is lower in the 2010s than in some earlier decades. Among the most common genres, Adventure, Science Fiction, and Action have relatively high mean popularity values. The genre comparison for adjusted profit also shows that financial results vary across genres for movies with reported budget and revenue data.

Limitations¶

There are a few limits to keep in mind when reading these results:

  1. Missing financial information: More than half of the original records have zero adjusted budget or adjusted revenue values. I treated those as missing, so the financial analysis only represents the movies with usable values.
  2. Popularity comes from TMDb: The popularity score is specific to the TMDb platform and should not be treated as a perfect measure of general audience interest.
  3. Genres overlap: A movie can have more than one genre, so the same movie can appear in several genre groups after the genre column is split.
  4. This is observational data: The patterns in this project are correlations. They do not prove that budget, popularity, or genre directly causes a movie to earn more revenue.
  5. Other factors are missing: Marketing, release strategy, competition, franchise status, and other outside factors can affect revenue and popularity but are not fully represented in this dataset.

Further research¶

If I continued this project, I would look at whether the budget and revenue relationship changes by decade. It could also be useful to compare return on investment while being careful with unusually small reported budgets, or to build a model that considers release year, genre, and popularity at the same time.
